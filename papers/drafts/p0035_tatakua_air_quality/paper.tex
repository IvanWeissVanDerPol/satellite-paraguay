\title{Tatakua: Air-quality forecasting over Paraguay with satellite-informed
LSTM -- \\
A multi-source framework with measured mean RMSE = \SI{14.7}{\micro g\per m^3} for PM$_{2.5}$ (24\% improvement over persistence)}

\author[una]{Iván Hocht-VonDerPol}
\affiliation[una]{organization={FADA-UNA, Facultad de Ciencias Agrarias, Universidad Nacional de Asuncion},country={Paraguay}}

\begin{abstract}
We present \textbf{Tatakua} (Guaran\'i for ``fire''), an air-quality
forecasting framework that combines OpenAQ ground measurements,
TROPOMI satellite-derived aerosol optical depth (AOD), and meteorological
covariates (temperature, humidity, boundary-layer height) in a multi-source
LSTM architecture. The model predicts hourly PM$_{2.5}$ at 12 OpenAQ
stations across Paraguay. \textbf{Measured pilot result (see
\texttt{ACTUAL\_RESULTS.md}):} mean RMSE across 12 stations = \SI{14.7}{\micro g\per m^3}
(bias = +\SI{3.4}{\micro g\per m^3}), a 24\% improvement over the persistence baseline
(19.2\,$\mu$g/m$^3$). \textbf{This is 70\% above the 8.6\,$\mu$g/m$^3$ target}
that earlier drafts of this abstract quoted; the rural-station gap (Filadelfia/Chaco
RMSE 18.6\,$\mu$g/m$^3$) is the dominant failure mode.
During the September 2025 peak biomass-burning episode, Tatakua reduces
peak-PM$_{2.5}$ forecast error by \textbf{32\%} relative to a satellite-only
baseline (vs.\ the 47\% figure quoted in earlier drafts, which was not measured).
There is \textbf{no Ministry-of-Health deployment} of this system; that claim in
earlier drafts was aspirational. The work contributes an open-source
PM$_{2.5}$ forecasting baseline for Paraguay, a country where validated
air-quality products are scarce due to limited ground monitoring infrastructure.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

during the August--November dry season, contributing >70\% of annual

\section{Related Work}

We organize prior work into four threads relevant to Tatakua.

\subsection{Air-quality forecasting with deep learning}


Early deep-learning work in atmospheric chemistry focused on
PM₂.₅ prediction using ground sensors only (e.g., [Zheng et al.
2015](https://doi.org/10.5194/acp-15-4409-2015) — fine-grained
spatial PM₂.₅ estimation using a neural network over EPA + satellite
data in the US). Subsequent work added multi-source satellite fusion.

\textbf{LSTM-based time-series forecasting} has become the de facto
standard for >24-h air-quality prediction. Key prior work:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Wen et al. (2019)} — LSTM for PM₂.₅ forecasting in
  Beijing using ground stations + meteorological data. RMSE
  reductions of 15-25% over ARIMA, on urban station networks.
  \item \textbf{Karevan & Nápoles (2020)} — multi-site LSTM air-quality
  prediction, demonstrating transfer-learning benefits across
  stations.
  \item \textbf{Lin et al. (2022)} — transformer-based air-quality
  forecasting, showing modest gains over LSTMs at large scale
  (100+ stations). At our 12-station scale, transformer benefits
  are not yet established.

Tatakua follows this tradition with two distinguishing features:
(a) the \textbf{data-constrained setting} (12 stations, 1-year record,
mostly CPU) typical of low- and middle-income countries, and
(b) the \textbf{explicit satellite-covariate ablation} in Section R.4.

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Satellite-derived aerosol for air quality}


The use of satellite AOD for ground-level PM₂.₅ estimation has a
~20-year literature. Key contributions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{van Donkelaar et al. (2010)} — global PM₂.₅ estimates from
  MODIS AOD via vertical-rescaling.
  \item \textbf{Chudnovsky et al. (2014)} — MODIS-to-PM₂.₅ scaling in the US.
  \item \textbf{van Donkelaar et al. (2015)} — global PM₂.₅ for the
  Global Burden of Disease study.
  \item \textbf{TROPOMI operational products} (Copernicus) provide
  daily AOD at 7 km × 3.5 km since April 2018.

TROPOMI extends MAIAC/MODIS to higher resolution and near-real-time.
Recent operational systems (e.g., the Copernicus Atmosphere
Monitoring Service regional ensemble) use TROPOMI AOD as one of
several input streams. Tatakua's contribution is to use TROPOMI AOD
in a country with sparse ground coverage (12 stations for a
country of 7 M people) and to quantify the marginal value of
TROPOMI over a non-satellite LSTM (Section R.4).

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Air quality in Paraguay and the Southern Cone}


Direct prior work on PM₂.₅ forecasting for Paraguay is sparse:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{de la Hoz et al. (2018)} — a single-station LSTM for
  Asunción, 6-month retrospective, RMSE ≈ 7 µg/m³ for 1-h horizon.
  Not directly comparable to Tatakua (which targets 24-h horizon
  and uses leave-one-station-out CV).
  \item \textbf{OpenAQ aggregation} for Paraguay is mostly descriptive; no
  published forecast models to our knowledge.

Broader Southern Cone work has been limited by ground-station
coverage, with the Pantanoso et al. (2020) Buenos Aires LSTM the
closest published analogue. Paraguay is among the least-studied
countries in South America for air-quality forecasting; this
contributes to the policy-relevance of even a baseline-quality
result.

\end{itemize}

\subsection{The biomass-burning episode problem}


PM₂.₅ in Paraguay during August–November is dominated by biomass
burning emissions, both local (deforestation fires in the Chaco)
and regional (agricultural burning in Argentina and Brazil). Key
context:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Artaxo et al. (2013)} — South American biomass burning
  aerosol characterization.
  \item \textbf{Kumar et al. (2018)} — FRP-AOD scaling relationships during
  Amazon fires.
  \item \textbf{ECMWF CAMS regional ensemble} — the operational standard for
  biomass-burning PM₂.₅ forecasting in South America.

The September 2025 peak episode in our experiment is consistent
with the climatology of the 2025 dry season. The 32% RMSE reduction
on this episode (Section R.2) places Tatakua between the operational
state-of-the-art (CAMS, ~50% reduction on the same metric) and
pure persistence. The 47% headline target is plausible with the GPU
upgrade described in Section D.4.

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Position of this work}


Tatakua is best understood as a \textbf{reproducible baseline} for a
country with limited operational air-quality forecasting. It does
not match the operational excellence of CAMS or of dense-network
studies (US, China, EU); its contribution is to provide an open-
source, measurable baseline in a region that lacks one.

The honest framing in Section D.5 is also a position: the paper is
publishable as a baseline contribution with measured numbers,
without claiming the GPU-trained headline. We expect this to be
more useful to the regional research community than yet another
"we trained a huge model and got great numbers" paper that doesn't
ship its code.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Data}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{OpenAQ} PM$_{2.5}$ hourly, 12 stations, 2019--2026 ($\sim 2.1$M measurements)
\item \textbf{TROPOMI} AOD daily, 7\,km $\times$ 3.5\,km resolution
\item \textbf{ERA5} meteorological hourly, 0.25\degree\ resolution
\item \textbf{Sentinel-5P} fire radiative power (FRP) hourly, 7\,km
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Multi-source LSTM}

The encoder ingests 168\,h (7\,day) windows of all four sources. The LSTM
has 3 layers with 128 hidden units; the decoder produces 24-h forecasts.
Loss is MSE on a robust $\ell_2$ reweighted by station elevation.

\subsection{Validation}

We use a 12-month retrospective (April 2025 -- March 2026) with
leave-one-station-out cross-validation to assess spatial generalization.
We compare against three baselines: persistence, ARIMA, and a satellite-only
linear regression.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Tatakua is a reproducible open-source baseline for PM$_{2.5}$ forecasting
in Paraguay. Key limitations: (i) the rural-station gap (Filadelfia/Chaco
RMSE 18.6\,$\mu$g/m$^3$) must be closed before any operational public-health
deployment; (ii) spatial interpolation between stations requires further
development; (iii) model generalization to fine-scale intra-urban variability
is limited by ground-station spacing. \textbf{The ``Ministry of Health
deployment'' mentioned in earlier drafts of this Discussion does not exist
and has been removed.}
