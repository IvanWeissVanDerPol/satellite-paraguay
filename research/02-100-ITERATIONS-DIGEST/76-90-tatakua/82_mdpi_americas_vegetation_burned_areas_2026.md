## Highlights

**What are the main findings?**

- Over 24 years, fires have been recorded in 44 countries and 269 ecoregions of the Americas, with a high recurrence of fires in the ecoregions of South America.

- There is significant spatial variability in vegetation dynamics after the fire, with some regions and land cover types showing increasing NDVI trends while others experience continuous declines.


**What is the implication of the main finding?**

- Understanding where and how vegetation changes after fire is essential for designing specific fire management and ecoregion restoration strategies.

- Coordinated policies and collaborative efforts are urgently needed to prevent repeated burns and ensure long-term resilience and ecological restoration of vulnerable and sensitive ecosystems across the entire American continent.


## Abstract

Fire is an essential component of species, ecosystems, and atmospheric dynamics. However, human activity has caused changes in fire regimes over the past two decades. In many cases, the spatial patterns of vegetation change after fire at the landscape scale remain unknown. The aim of this study was to evaluate spatial vegetation trends in burned areas across the Americas (2001–2024), using non-parametric tests and analyzing Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) remote sensing products. Over a period of 24 years, fire activity burned a total area of 429.7 million hectares in 44 countries or territories and 269 ecoregions in the Americas. Regarding fire recurrence, the data indicates that 244.7 Mha (56.9%) burned only once (≤1), while 185.0 Mha (43.1%) burned multiple times (≥2), with certain regions experiencing up to 39 fires. The NDVI trend analysis showed that burned areas with increasing trends (p < 0.05) represented a total of 149.6 Mha (34.8%), primarily in Brazil (54.6 Mha, 12.7%), Argentina (17.8 Mha, 4.2%), the United States (14.4 Mha, 3.4%). In terms of decreasing NDVI trends (p < 0.05), these represented a total of 91.8 Mha (21.37%), primarily in Brazil (29.1 Mha, 6.8%), Canada (23.4 Mha, 5.4%), and the United States (14.2 Mha, 3.3%). The ecoregions with the largest areas showing increasing NDVI trends (p < 0.05) were the Cerrado (33.8 Mha, 7.8%), the Llanos (13.3 Mha, 3.1%) and the Humid Chaco (7 Mha, 1.6%). In contrast, the ecoregions with the largest areas showing decreasing NDVI trends (p < 0.05) were the Dry Chaco (9.2 Mha, 2.1%), the Cerrado (8.6 Mha, 2.0%), and the Boreal Shield (8.3 Mha, 1.9%). In terms of land cover types, savannas (37.2%) exhibited the highest proportions of increasing NDVI trends (p < 0.05), while decreasing trends were also present in savannas (28.0%) and grasslands (22.1%). Identifying spatiotemporal trends in vegetation change after fires is a fundamental step in implementing strategies and public policies to ensure ecological restoration. Moreover, given the high costs of restoration efforts, governments must work together to prevent these ecosystems from burning repeatedly.

## 1\. Introduction

Fire is a globally widespread element and plays a fundamental role in the composition and structure of terrestrial ecosystems, as well as in the distribution of different species \[ [1](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B1-remotesensing-18-01870), [2](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B2-remotesensing-18-01870), [3](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B3-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. The conditions that determine the occurrence of fires are influenced by various factors, including the amount, composition, and moisture content of the fuel, the presence of an ignition source, and climatic conditions \[ [4](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B4-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Despite the existence of natural fires (e.g., lightning-caused) \[ [5](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B5-remotesensing-18-01870)\], human activity has emerged as the primary driver (e.g., cultural burning), both directly and indirectly, in igniting fires in various ecosystems \[ [6](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B6-remotesensing-18-01870), [7](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B7-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Historically, various civilizations have driven changes in fire activity \[ [8](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B8-remotesensing-18-01870), [9](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B9-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. In recent decades, transformations induced by human activity and climate change have altered fire regimes, causing fire to become a key driver of ecosystem change \[ [10](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B10-remotesensing-18-01870), [11](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B11-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. However, the area burned worldwide decreased by 26% between 2002 and 2021 \[ [12](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B12-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Although this reduction in the burned area appears to be associated with the expansion and intensification of agriculture in savannas and grasslands, in tropical forests there is evidence of an increase in fire associated with population density and agricultural lands \[ [13](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B13-remotesensing-18-01870)\].

In the Americas, several types of ecosystems are adapted to the presence of fire and depend on ecosystem-specific fire regimes for their regeneration and maintenance \[ [14](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B14-remotesensing-18-01870), [15](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B15-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Savanna and grassland landscapes are shaped by burning and form part of the ecology of these ecosystems \[ [16](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B16-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Plant species exhibit adaptive traits under a particular fire regime, such as the ability to resprout, seed bank persistence, or the ability to grow or disperse \[ [6](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B6-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. In addition, some tree species develop tick and corky bark, which increases with stem diameter and provides tolerance to high temperatures during fire \[ [17](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B17-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. However, evidence has been found that fires, in systemic interaction with heat and drought, can threaten the resilience of ecosystems that are, in principle, even adapted to fire \[ [18](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B18-remotesensing-18-01870)\].

Fire is one of the main causes of forest degradation, especially in the tropics \[ [19](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B19-remotesensing-18-01870)\], with high tree mortality \[ [20](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B20-remotesensing-18-01870)\], as these ecosystems are more sensitive due to biomass concentration and structural complexity. Forest fires have been responsible for degrading large forest areas in several regions of the American continent \[ [21](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B21-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Recent studies show that the fires of 2024 and 2025 in the Americas have been particularly anomalous, predominantly affecting the boreal forests of North America, tropical rainforests, tropical dry forests, the grasslands, savannas and wetlands of South America \[ [22](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B22-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Moreover, South America is becoming warmer, drier, and more prone to extreme fires, with an accelerated trend since the late 20th century \[ [23](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B23-remotesensing-18-01870)\].

The dynamics of recovery after a fire are a key component of ecosystem resilience and stability. In this regard, it is necessary to prioritize ecological restoration measures in sites impacted by fire \[ [24](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B24-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. The expected benefits of these restoration actions include biodiversity recovery, carbon sequestration and storage, improved ecosystem services (e.g., water regulation, erosion reduction), as well as increased agricultural and forestry productivity, which generates local income opportunities and improves resilience to climate change \[ [25](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B25-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Monitoring vegetation change after a fire is essential for assessing resilience and guiding management and restoration \[ [26](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B26-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. However, in many cases, landscape-scale recovery processes after a fire are unknown, especially at the scale of countries or territories and ecoregions. While it is essential to understand processes at the plot scale, long-term, landscape-scale analyses are needed, considering the different types of ecoregions in the Americas. In this regard, it is necessary to use available technologies to analyze vegetation dynamics in the context of post-fire recovery processes.

The use of modern Earth observation techniques for multiscale and multitemporal monitoring is essential for assessing vegetation change, allowing monitoring across large areas and different ecosystems \[ [26](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B26-remotesensing-18-01870), [27](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B27-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. A series of remote sensing-based methodologies have been proposed for postfire monitoring. Some of these methodologies evaluate spectral trajectories using different indices, metrics, and temporal perspectives of fire \[ [28](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B28-remotesensing-18-01870), [29](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B29-remotesensing-18-01870), [30](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B30-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Spectral indices derived from satellite products (e.g., NDVI) are the most commonly used to monitor vegetation change with continuous time series analysis approaches, although there are also metrics derived from spectral mixture analysis, geographic object-based analysis, and digital image classification techniques \[ [31](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B31-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Although some of these spectral indices do not identify the structure, composition, or biomass of vegetation, they are widely used as proxies of post-fire photosynthetic recovery and canopy greenness rather than full ecosystem recovery \[ [32](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B32-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. This research carried out a comprehensive and large-scale quantitative evaluation of the vegetation trends in burned areas across the American continent during the period from 2001 to 2024, relying on remote sensing data. The research questions that guided our study are:

(i)

What were the spatiotemporal dynamics of fires in terms of burned area and fire recurrence?

(ii)

What were the dynamics of post-fire vegetation?

These questions were analyzed across 52 countries or territories, 280 ecoregions, and 14 land cover types. This research aims to serve as reference information for future monitoring efforts and to facilitate the development of effective strategies to monitor and promote vegetation recovery after fires.

## 2\. Materials and Methods

### 2.1. Study Area

The American continent extends from 83.65°N to 55.98°S, and from 34.47°W to 172.27°E, covering an area of almost 4196 million hectares (Mha). The topography of the Americas is diverse and consists of mountain ranges (e.g., the Rocky Mountains, the Andes), plateaus (e.g., the Mexican Plateau, the Altiplano), plains (e.g., the Amazon basin), depressions (e.g., the Great basin), large coastal microcontinents (e.g., Greenland), islands (e.g., the Antilles, the Canadian Arctic Archipelago), and peripheral islands (e.g., the Galapagos, the Aleutians). The wide altitudinal gradient, which varies in altitude and climatic characteristics, determines a great variety of biomes, ecoregions, and vegetation formations. Around 52 country or territory borders have been administratively recognized across the continental landmass and islands. Furthermore, 280 terrestrial and freshwater ecoregions have been identified \[ [33](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B33-remotesensing-18-01870)\], encompassing the biomes of tundra, boreal forest, temperate forests, subtropical forests, tropical forests, savannas, grasslands, deserts, and wetlands. Many of these ecoregions are affected by fire, but the post-fire natural regeneration processes differ substantially depending on the ecological context. The rate and trajectory of recovery are influenced by factors such as vegetation structure and composition, as well as fire intensity and recurrence. (see [Figure 1](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#remotesensing-18-01870-f001)).

### 2.2. Datasets

Three remote sensing datasets were used in this study, namely burned areas (MCD64A1), vegetation index (MOD13A1), and land use and land cover (MCD12Q1). The following sections describe these products, as well as the data processing and corresponding statistical analyses.

### 2.3. Burned Areas

The identification of burned areas was based on the use of the monthly MCD64A1 version 6.1 product, which uses a combination of Terra and Aqua (MODIS) satellites with a spatial resolution of 500 m \[ [34](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B34-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. This product is generated by an automated system that identifies rapid patterns in the variations in surface reflectivity time series captured by MODIS sensors. The capabilities of the system extend to the reduction in unmapped areas in regions with high cloud cover, as well as to the improvement in the detection of small fires \[ [35](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B35-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. For this study, monthly data were used to identify fire recurrence, since in some areas fires occur more than once a year.

### 2.4. Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI)

The Normalized Difference Vegetation Index is a measure used to assess the health and density of vegetation in a given area. This index is calculated from the reflectance of light in the near-infrared and visible red bands, which allows for the distinction between vegetated and non-vegetated surfaces \[ [36](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B36-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. NDVI values range from −1 to +1, with values close to +1 indicating dense, healthy vegetation, while values close to 0 suggest the presence of bare soil or areas without vegetation \[ [37](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B37-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Negative values generally correspond to water surfaces or clouds. In this study, NDVI was used strictly as an indicator of green vegetation cover, without extrapolating its meaning to other ecological properties such as aboveground biomass or productivity, since NDVI is influenced primarily by vegetation greenness and is limited in capturing structural or functional ecosystem changes \[ [38](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B38-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. To analyze temporal trends in green vegetation cover, we used the MOD13A2 Version 6 product, which provides NDVI values every 16 days at a 500 m spatial resolution \[ [39](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B39-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. This product enables consistent spatial and temporal comparisons of vegetation conditions at continental scales, and is derived from MODIS L3 daily surface reflectance, corrected for atmospheric effects, thin cirrus clouds, and aerosols \[ [39](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B39-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. While higher-resolution datasets (e.g., Landsat, Sentinel-2) offer finer spatial detail, MODIS was chosen for its long-term data continuity, high temporal frequency, and comprehensive coverage, which are essential for large-scale, multi-year analyses \[ [40](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B40-remotesensing-18-01870)\].

### 2.5. Land Cover Type

We evaluate the type of vegetation burned using the MODIS Land Cover Type MCD12Q1 product, a series of global land cover maps with annual intervals and a spatial resolution of 500 m. This product was developed based on supervised classifications of MODIS Terra and Aqua reflectance data \[ [41](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B41-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. For this study, we used the University of Maryland (UMD) classification (Type 2) of the MCD12Q1.006 product because its spatial resolution integrates with other MODIS products, the classification covers the entire American continent, and it has a broad temporal scale. For this study, 14 land cover types were identified in which fire events were recorded: Water Bodies, Evergreen Needleleaf Forests, Evergreen Broadleaf Forests, Deciduous Needleleaf Forests, Deciduous Broadleaf Forests, Mixed Forests, Closed Shrublands, Open Shrublands, Woody Savannas, Savannas, Grasslands, Cropland, Urban and Built-up Lands, and Non-Vegetated Lands.

### 2.6. Data Processing and Statistical Analyses

Using the cloud computing platform Google Earth Engine (GEE, \[ [42](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B42-remotesensing-18-01870)\]), we developed scripts to generate annual cumulative images of the NDVI time series and detect trend changes between 2001 and 2024. For burned areas, we analyzed the NDVI series specifically from the post-fire period. The NDVI time series was constructed by computing the monthly median NDVI for each 500 m pixel, using all available observations within each month. Images affected by cloud cover were excluded based on standard GEE quality masks, thereby minimizing atmospheric and sensor noise. This approach also helps buffer short-term fluctuations due to phenological shifts and annual climate variability. To detect trends in NDVI values, we used a modified version of the Mann–Kendall nonparametric statistic, which returns corrected p-values after considering the temporal pseudoreplication of the variables analyzed \[ [43](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B43-remotesensing-18-01870), [44](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B44-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. The Mann–Kendall test is a non-parametric test used mainly to determine statistically significant monotonic trends in a time series of Z-scores \[ [45](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B45-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Each time step was compared to the previous step to evaluate the temporal trends. The sum of all the pairwise signs is what is known as the Mann–Kendall trend \[ [45](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B45-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. The result was +1 if the Z score at the second time step was greater than the Z score at the first time step, −1 if the opposite is true, and zero if they were both equal \[ [46](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B46-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Over the 23-year period, each pair of time steps was compared, obtaining the Mann–Kendall statistic with the associated trend Z-score and p-value for each pixel. Significant trends were defined as those below the threshold p-values of 0.05. To facilitate a direct comparison between different regions, we presented results using four categories of trends: positive and significant (p < 0.05), positive and non-significant (p \> 0.05), negative and non-significant (p \> 0.05), and negative and significant (p < 0.05). Positive values indicate an increasing trend in vegetation greenness and negative values indicate a decreasing trend.

We also developed a script in GEE to generate and download the accumulated scars of burned areas for each year (2001–2024) based on the monthly MCD64A1 product, and another script for the annual MCD12Q1.006 product on land cover types. We used the results of fire recurrences and intersected them with country or territory boundaries \[ [47](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B47-remotesensing-18-01870)\] and ecoregions \[ [33](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B33-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. In addition, fire recurrence data and Mann–Kendall test results were intersected with the net changes in land cover types between 2001 and 2024 at the continental scale. Finally, we classified the four spatio-temporal change trends and their levels of statistical significance (p < 0.05; p > 0.05), and obtained the areas (hectares) and percentages for burned areas in the different countries or territories and ecoregions of the Americas.

## 3\. Results

### 3.1. Fire Recurrence by Countries or Territories

Over a period of 24 years, from Alaska to Patagonia, fires have been recorded in 44 of the 52 countries or territories in the Americas and the Caribbean ( [Table S1](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#app1-remotesensing-18-01870)). No fire events were detected in the remaining 8 countries or territories. The results showed that the burned areas reached a total absolute surface area of 429.7 million hectares (Mha), equivalent to 10.2% of the entire continent. Fires occured mainly in Brazil (154.1 Mha, 35.9%), the United States (57.5 Mha, 13.4%), Canada (57.1 Mha, 13.3%), Argentina (43.2 Mha, 10.1%), Bolivia (29 Mha, 6.8%), Mexico (19.9 Mha, 4.6%), Venezuela (18.9 Mha, 4.4%), Paraguay (18.5 Mha, 4.3%), Colombia (13.3 Mha, 3.1%) and Honduras (2.8 Mha, 0.7%) ( [Table S1](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#app1-remotesensing-18-01870)). The burned areas were mainly concentrated in the central-eastern region of South America. Considering the percentage of each country’s total land area that burned, the highest values were recorded in Paraguay (46.5%), Bolivia (26.6%), Honduras (25.3%), Guatemala (22.7%) y Venezuela (20.7%) ( [Table S1](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#app1-remotesensing-18-01870)). The data also indicates that 4196.4 Mha (89.8%) of the continent remained unburned between 2001 and 2024, including some islands (e.g., Aruba, Antigua and Barbuda, Bermuda). Regarding fire recurrence, the data indicates that 244.7 Mha (56.9%) burned only once (≤1), while 185.0 Mha (43.1%) burned multiple times (≥2), with certain regions experiencing up to 39 fires ( [Figure 2](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#remotesensing-18-01870-f002), [Table S2](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#app1-remotesensing-18-01870)). The countries with a fire recurrence rate exceeding 60% were Bolivia (18.6 Mha, 64.1%), Colombia (8.5 Mha, 63.8%), Paraguay (11.5 Mha, 62.1%), Venezuela (11.6 Mha, 61.6%), and the Bahamas (0.1 Mha, 60.5%) ( [Figure 2](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#remotesensing-18-01870-f002), [Table S2](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#app1-remotesensing-18-01870)).

### 3.2. Fire Recurrence by Ecoregions

At least 269 of 280 (96%) ecoregions in the Americas experienced fires. No fire events were detected in the remaining 11 ecoregions. The results indicated that, at the continental scale, fires are mainly recorded in the Cerrado (75.9 Mha, 17.7%), Llanos (21.6 Mha, 5.0%), Dry Chaco (20.1 Mha, 4.7%), Humid Chaco (17.4 Mha, 4.1%), Boreal Shield (17.1 Mha, 4.0%), Mato Grosso Seasonal Forests (13.8 Mha, 3.2%), Pantanal (11.2 Mha, 2.6%), Madeira-Tapajós Moist Forests (10.5 Mha, 2.5%), Chiquitano Dry Forests (10.3 Mha, 2.4%), and Alto Paraná Atlantic Forests (10.0 Mha, 2.3%) ( [Table S3](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#app1-remotesensing-18-01870)). Considering the percentage of each ecoregion’s total land area that burned, the highest values were recorded in Beni Savanna (70.7%), Pantanal (65.6%), Humid Chaco (59.9%), Miskito Pine Forests (59.3%), and Llanos (57.3%). Regarding fire recurrence, the ecoregions that experienced multiple fires (≥2) included Beni Savanna (7.2 Mha, 81.2%), Miskito Pine Forests (0.8 Mha, 76.7%), Pantanal (8.2 Mha, 73.4%), Llanos (15.8 Mha, 73.3%), and Humid Chaco (11.6 Mha, 66.8%) ( [Figure 3](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#remotesensing-18-01870-f003) and [Figure 4](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#remotesensing-18-01870-f004), [Table S4](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#app1-remotesensing-18-01870)).

### 3.3. Fire Recurrence by Land Cover Type

Over a period of 24 years, 67.9% of burned areas across the American continent experienced no change in land cover type. However, in the remaining 32.1%, the main net changes have been in the conversion between different types of non-forest cover (17.5%; grasslands, savannas, wooded savannas), forest to non-forest (7.2%), non-forest to forest (2.8%), and non-forest to cropland (2.4%), followed by other changes in lesser proportions ( [Table S5](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#app1-remotesensing-18-01870)). Currently, burned areas are mainly concentrated in savannas (131.5 Mha, 33.2%), followed by grasslands (102.4 Mha, 25.9%) and wooded savannas (54.8 Mha, 13.8%) ( [Table S6](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#app1-remotesensing-18-01870)). In terms of fire recurrence by country or territory, areas burned multiple times (≥2) were recorded in the savannas (35.6 Mha) and grasslands (26.0 Mha) of Brazil ( [Table S6](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#app1-remotesensing-18-01870)). In terms of ecoregions, fire recurrences (≥2) were mainly recorded in savannas (22.1 Mha) and grasslands (17.3 Mha) of Cerrado ( [Figure 5](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#remotesensing-18-01870-f005) and [Figure 6](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#remotesensing-18-01870-f006), [Table S7](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#app1-remotesensing-18-01870)).

### 3.4. Spatiotemporal Trends of NDVI by Countries or Territories

The NDVI trends during 24 years of fires in the Americas showed various spatial variations ( [Figure 7](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#remotesensing-18-01870-f007) and [Figure 8](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#remotesensing-18-01870-f008)). The results showed evidence that, on a continental scale, burned areas with increasing trends in NDVI values (p < 0.05) represented a total of 149.6 Mha (34.8%), mainly in Brazil (54.6 Mha, 12.7%), Argentina (17.8 Mha, 4.2%), United States (14.4 Mha, 3.4%), Bolivia (12.0 Mha, 2.8%), and Mexico (10.3 Mha, 2.4%) ( [Figure 7](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#remotesensing-18-01870-f007) and [Figure 8](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#remotesensing-18-01870-f008), [Table S8](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#app1-remotesensing-18-01870)). The rest of the burned areas with increasing (27.3%) and decreasing trends (16.5%) were non-statistically significant.

The results indicated that, across a continental scale, areas affected by fire exhibiting increasing trends in NDVI values were predominantly located in savannas (p < 0.05, 37.2%; p > 0.05, 34.1%), grasslands (p < 0.05, 29.1%; p > 0.05, 26.4%), and wooded savannas (p < 0.05, 9.7%; p \> 0.05, 12.0%) ( [Figure 12](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#remotesensing-18-01870-f012)). ## 4\. Discussion

### 4.1. Fire Recurrence by Countries or Territories

This study analyzed long-term burned areas by countries or territories across the Americas. Results indicate that fires were recorded in 10.2% of the entire continent, mainly in Brazil, the United States, and Canada. In Brazil, as in other South American countries, fire is associated with slash-and-burn systems practiced by subsistence farmers, deforestation for future agricultural use, and the management of agricultural or grazing systems \[ [48](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B48-remotesensing-18-01870), [49](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B49-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. However, recent studies also suggest that political and institutional changes during the administration of 2019–2022 may have contributed to increased fire activity by weakening environmental governance and enforcement \[ [50](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B50-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. In the case of the United States, human-caused wildfires accounted for 84% of all wildfires (1992–2012) and were responsible for nearly half of the total area burned \[ [51](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B51-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Recent studies have identified an increase in rapid daily fires in the western United States (2001–2020), due to a combination of climate change factors, extreme weather conditions, the presence of more flammable fuels, a greater number of human ignitions, urban expansion in risk areas without adequate planning, and historical fire management and land use policies \[ [52](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B52-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. In Canada, approximately half of all fires are identified as being started by humans, while lightning strikes are responsible for a larger proportion of the area burned \[ [53](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B53-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Furthermore, 2023 was a record year in terms of forest area affected by fires in Canada, with 14.6 Mha attributed to several environmental factors that converged at the start of the season, such as early snowmelt and drought conditions \[ [54](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B54-remotesensing-18-01870)\].

The results indicate that Paraguay, Bolivia, and Honduras were the countries with the highest proportion of burned area in relation to the size of their territory. In addition, Bolivia, Colombia, and Paraguay were identified as having the largest areas burned multiple times. In Paraguay, which has historically experienced a very high environmental and social impact \[ [55](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B55-remotesensing-18-01870)\], wildfires have affected 46.5% of its territory. The causes of fires in Paraguay are related to deforestation in seasonal dry forests \[ [56](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B56-remotesensing-18-01870)\] due to land conversion for livestock and agriculture, mainly for soybean cultivation \[ [57](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B57-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Bolivia, the country with the second-highest proportion of burned area (26.6%) and the highest recurrence of fires (64.1%), has experienced large-scale forest fires due to poor fire management during traditional subsistence farming practices, mechanized commercial agriculture, and public policies that encourage the expansion of the agricultural frontier \[ [57](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B57-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. In addition, 2024 was a record year in terms of area affected by fires in Bolivia, with nearly 11 Mha \[ [58](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B58-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. In Honduras, fires have been identified as one of the most serious threats during the dry season (December to April) and among the main causes of forest and habitat loss \[ [59](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B59-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. In Colombia, fires have been reported most frequently in the eastern plains, followed by the Amazon and Caribbean regions \[ [60](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B60-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Recent analyses have identified record fire anomalies in some regions of Colombia in 2024–2025 \[ [22](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B22-remotesensing-18-01870)\].

The persistent recurrence of fires across the Americas underscores the need for comprehensive public policies that address both environmental and socio-economic factors. For example, recurrent fires and their spread in less developed countries have a significant impact on livelihoods, leading to increased poverty among the most vulnerable populations, as in the case of Bolivia \[ [61](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B61-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Integrated fire management should prioritize sustainable land-use planning, stricter regulation of agricultural expansion, and investment in early warning and monitoring systems. Strengthening institutional capacities, promoting cross-sector collaboration, and engaging local communities and indigenous groups are crucial. Adaptive, evidence-based policies will be essential to reduce wildfire risks, preserve ecosystem services, and promote long-term sustainability in fire-affected regions.

### 4.2. Fire Recurrence by Ecoregions

This study also reports that 96% of America’s ecoregions have experienced fires, concentrated mainly in Cerrado, Llanos, and Dry Chaco. The Cerrado of Brazil and Bolivia accounts for 17.7% of the total area burned on the continent. The Cerrado is the most diverse savanna in the world, harboring plant and animal species that are adapted to frequent, rapid, low-intensity fires \[ [15](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B15-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. During the wet season, there is high biomass production, which dries out as the dry season progresses, favoring the occurrence of fires \[ [62](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B62-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. However, recent studies have found substantial changes in the number and shape of regions with fire regimes, as well as in their characteristics \[ [63](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B63-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Moreover, it has been found that livestock density can directly suppress fire activity in the Brazilian Cerrado by reducing the amount of fuel or altering fire management decisions \[ [13](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B13-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. These findings reinforce growing concerns that grassy ecoregions in South America are undergoing rapid ecological changes and remain underrecognized in conservation policies, despite their biodiversity and dependence on natural disturbance regimes such as fire \[ [64](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B64-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. The Llanos of Venezuela and Colombia represent 5.0% of the burned areas in the Americas. This ecoregion is characterized by a mosaic of savannas, wetlands, and different types of forests \[ [65](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B65-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Furthermore, the Dry Chaco of Paraguay, Argentina, and Bolivia, accounted for 4.7% of the burned area. The Dry Chaco consists of a mixture of forests, savannas, and grasslands, with a unimodal seasonality in terms of fires (winter-spring) \[ [66](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B66-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. This ecoregion has become a global hotspot for deforestation and land-use change \[ [67](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B67-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Recently, studies have shown that most fires in the Dry Chaco are caused by land clearing for agricultural expansion and by fires used for crop or pasture management \[ [56](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B56-remotesensing-18-01870)\].

The results indicate that Beni Savanna, Pantanal, and Humid Chaco were the ecoregions with the highest proportion of burned area relative to total area. In addition, Beni Savanna, Miskito Pine Forests, and Pantanal were identified as the areas with the largest burned area on multiple occasions. Between 2012 and 2020, 77% of the burned wetland areas in South America were concentrated in Pantanal, Cerrado, Llanos, Beni Savanna, Humid Chaco, and Dry Chaco \[ [68](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B68-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Beni Savanna (known as Llanos de Moxos) located in the Beni-Mamoré-Iténez basin in the southwestern Amazon in Bolivia, represents the ecoregion with the second highest proportion of burned area (70.7%) and the highest recurrence of fires (81.2%). It is a seasonally flooded tropical savanna containing a complex mosaic of small islands of palm trees and gallery forests \[ [69](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B69-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Ecologically, fires are fundamental to the dynamics of this savanna ecosystem \[ [70](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B70-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. The Pantanal, located in Brazil, Bolivia, and Paraguay, is the largest tropical wetland in the world. In 2020, unprecedented fires were recorded in the Pantanal, linked to severe droughts, heat waves, temperature anomalies, and the improper and irresponsible use of fire \[ [71](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B71-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Moreover, the 2024 fire season in the Pantanal was the result of a compound event of different factors, including the most extensive and severe drought of the period 1980–2024 \[ [72](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B72-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. These catastrophic wildfires have also acted as catalysts for institutional and community-level changes in fire governance and adaptive management, highlighting the profound socio-ecological impacts of extreme fire events in the wetland \[ [73](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B73-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. In the case of the Humid Chaco, located in Paraguay and Argentina, the native vegetation is composed of a mosaic of forest, savanna, and palm wetlands \[ [74](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B74-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. This ecoregion shows bimodal seasonality in terms of fires (summer-autumn and winter-spring) \[ [66](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B66-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. The abundant rainfall regimes lead to a high accumulation of biomass which, under extreme summer temperatures, provides a large amount of dry fuel that can facilitate the spread of fire \[ [75](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B75-remotesensing-18-01870)\].

The complex interaction of ecological adaptation and human activities is shown by the persistent fires in the Americas. While some ecosystems are fire-adapted, land-use changes and climate anomalies threaten biodiversity and ecosystem resilience by changing fire regimes. These issues require targeted research, improved fire monitoring, and ecoregion-specific management strategies. Particularly for the dry forest regions of South America (e.g., Cerrado, Chiquitano Dry Forest, Chaco), many of which are experiencing increased agricultural expansion and intensification \[ [76](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B76-remotesensing-18-01870)\] and increased burning \[ [77](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B77-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Conserving ecosystem services and reducing wildfire impacts continent-wide requires increased collaboration and knowledge exchange.

### 4.3. Fire Recurrence by Land Cover Type

The results show that, over a 24-year period, 32.1% of the burned areas in the Americas experienced changes in land cover type, especially among different non-forest formations such as savannas, grasslands, and wooded savannas. Moreover, land cover transitions, particularly from forests to non-forest formations and agricultural lands after fires, are closely related to the recurrence of fires and highlight the complex interaction between fire, vegetation dynamics, and human activities. Recurrent fires have promoted the diversification of land cover, transforming areas originally covered by forests or shrub associations into agricultural lands or areas with sparse vegetation \[ [78](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B78-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. A recent study based on remote sensing data identify that more than half of the area burned by recurrent fires in South America between 2001 and 2020 was concentrated mainly in savannas, while the greatest conversion of forests to agricultural land coincided with a higher number of recurrent fires \[ [79](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B79-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Additionally, evidence of changes in forest cover types has been found in burned areas in the southwestern United States, where coniferous forests are being replaced by broadleaf trees and shrubs \[ [80](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B80-remotesensing-18-01870)\].

Savannas and grasslands, mainly in the Cerrado region of Brazil, had the largest area. The recurrence of fires is strongly influenced by the structure and composition of the vegetation, as well as by the characteristics of the fuel \[ [81](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B81-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. In this sense, the different types of vegetation that make up the continent’s ecoregions and biomes have different fire regimes \[ [82](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B82-remotesensing-18-01870), [83](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B83-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. In addition, both the types of land cover present and landscape management practices influence the probability and frequency of recurrent fires \[ [84](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B84-remotesensing-18-01870), [85](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B85-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. The future risk of forest fires, especially after mass tree mortality, shows a decrease in the potential for crown fires, but an increase in the intensity of surface fires in the short and medium term, due to the accumulation of dry woody material and large combustible material, which could trigger massive and severe fires in the coming decades \[ [86](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B86-remotesensing-18-01870), [87](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B87-remotesensing-18-01870), [88](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B88-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Although the recurrence of fires is decisive for degradation and changes in cover, from an ecological perspective, grasslands and savannas are considered fire-dependent, showing specific adaptations and interactions \[ [3](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B3-remotesensing-18-01870), [89](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B89-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. In western North America, indigenous land management employed fire; but, during the past 200 years, fire suppression strategies have led to an accumulation of fuels and increased fire intensity \[ [90](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B90-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. This situation is currently aggravated by drought \[ [91](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B91-remotesensing-18-01870)\] and beetle infestations \[ [92](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B92-remotesensing-18-01870)\], leading to heightened tree mortality \[ [93](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B93-remotesensing-18-01870)\] and fuel loads \[ [94](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B94-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. The function of fire is intricate; nonetheless, the practices of thinning and planned burns are increasingly favored for forest management and the mitigation of catastrophic fire incidents \[ [95](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B95-remotesensing-18-01870)\].

Analysis of fire recurrence by land cover type highlights the complexity and dynamics of interactions between fire, vegetation, and human activities in ecosystems across the Americas. The changes observed in land cover, especially in savannas and grasslands, reflect not only the direct influence of recurrent fire, but also the way in which landscape management practices and vegetation composition determine the frequency and impact of fires. These results highlight the need for differentiated approaches to fire management, considering that some ecosystems have specific adaptations and responses to fire, while others suffer significant degradation.

### 4.4. Spatiotemporal Trends of NDVI by Countries or Territories

This study analyzed recent seasonal spatial trends of vegetation in burned areas in the Americas. The results indicate that significant increasing trends in NDVI values were recorded in 34.8% of the entire continent, mainly in Brazil, Argentina, and the United States. Additionally, it was observed that 21.37% of the burned areas show significant decreasing trends in NDVI values and are mainly located in Brazil, Canada, and the United States. These results allow for prioritizing actions to recover areas with declining vegetation trends, especially forested areas. Although South American forests are less susceptible to recurring fires, the loss of vegetation cover is greater than in savannas and croplands, which have a higher incidence of fires \[ [79](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B79-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. The governments and authorities of the countries in the Americas have a huge task ahead of them to fulfill the commitments made as part of the global climate agenda and a fundamental part of the ambitious international goals \[ [96](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B96-remotesensing-18-01870)\], including the Strategic Plan for Biodiversity, the Aichi Biodiversity Target 15, the Bonn Challenge, Target 5 of the New York Declaration on Forests \[ [97](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B97-remotesensing-18-01870)\], and the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework \[ [98](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B98-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Consequently, efforts to restore forests have been formally recognized as a national objective in numerous countries, with legal provisions established to support these initiatives \[ [99](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B99-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. For example, Brazil has made progress with the national plan for the recovery of native vegetation (PLANAVEG), which aims to restore 12 million hectares as part of climate and biodiversity commitments \[ [100](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B100-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. However, it is necessary to address issues related to sustainable financing, the removal of obstacles in governance and land ownership, the effective inclusion of local communities in the decision-making process, and the management of potential conflicts between productive and conservation objectives \[ [25](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B25-remotesensing-18-01870)\].

The restoration of forest landscapes should be viewed as a sustained, long-term commitment rather than a short-term or immediate response to forest fires. While prompt action is necessary to protect remaining biodiversity, stabilize ecosystems, and reduce erosion, only a continuous and strategic approach can achieve comprehensive and lasting recovery \[ [101](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B101-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Fundamental to this process are the restoration of forest landscapes, the reconstruction of habitats for key species, restore connectivity between habitat patches and the recovery of essential ecosystem services \[ [102](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B102-remotesensing-18-01870), [103](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B103-remotesensing-18-01870), [104](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B104-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Achieving these goals requires careful planning, a broad vision, and persistent efforts that span decades. However, real progress depends on the integration and collaboration of all relevant stakeholders, including governments, local communities, researchers, and the private sector, working collectively toward shared objectives. It is crucial to implement projects that not only address ecosystem restoration but also ensure their sustainable management over time \[ [105](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B105-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. This entails investing in environmental education, fostering scientific research, and enacting public policies that support ongoing conservation well beyond emergency scenarios. In this context, territorial governance platforms (p.e., Latin American Model Forest Network) and large-scale regional efforts (p.e., Initiative 20 × 20) exemplify how landscape-scale restoration can be promoted through participatory and adaptive management processes that integrate ecological restoration, sustainable production, and community livelihoods. These approaches, which rely on long-term commitments and multi-stakeholder collaboration, effectively mobilize governments, civil society, and the private sector toward ambitious forest landscape restoration targets, thereby strengthening long-term resilience beyond post-fire recovery.

### 4.5. Spatiotemporal Trends of NDVI by Ecoregions

This study shows that the ecoregions with the largest extent of significant increasing trends in NDVI values were Cerrado, Llanos, and Humid Chaco, while Dry Chaco, Cerrado, and Boreal Shield were those with the largest extent of decreasing trends. It is well known that fire plays a crucial role in regeneration in the Cerrado. Plants in this ecoregion have mechanisms for rapid regrowth and intense post-fire flowering, as well as morphological adaptations such as protected buds, well-developed roots and underground organs, while trees have thick, insulating bark \[ [15](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B15-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. However, the results of this study identify negative trends in NDVI values in the Cerrado. In recent years, degradation of this ecoregion has increased due to land use change and alterations in the fire regime \[ [106](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B106-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. The plains feature grass species with high regrowth capacity and pyrophytic trees with fire-resistant bark and enlarged underground structures \[ [65](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B65-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. It has also been reported that the frequency and timing of fires affect the structure and species composition of woody plant communities in this ecoregion \[ [62](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B62-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Moreover, it was evidenced that annual fires (over 15 years) decreased the diversity, richness, stem density, and carbon flux and reserves and productivity in the Cerrado, promoting a less complex and less fire-resistant ecoregion \[ [107](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B107-remotesensing-18-01870), [108](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B108-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Resprouting is a common phenomenon in dry ecosystems, regarded as one of the key mechanisms for natural regeneration \[ [109](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B109-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. In the case of the Dry Chaco and Humid Chaco, woody species in native forests and savanna trees have different strategies for surviving fire, mainly through vegetative regeneration \[ [110](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B110-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. In the Boreal Shield, it has been identified that both upper canopy and understory plants are well adapted to fire disturbances, employing a variety of strategies for survival or recolonization \[ [111](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B111-remotesensing-18-01870)\].

### 4.6. Spatiotemporal Trends of NDVI by Land Cover Type

This study reveals that both the increase and decrease in NDVI values are primarily found in savanna, grassland, and wooded savanna formations, which account for 72.9% of the burned areas across the continent. As fire is a natural disturbance in many ecosystems, native vegetation in these areas is often well-adapted to regenerate following wildfires. Many plant species in these ecosystems have developed morphological and physiological adaptations that not only enable them to survive fire but also use it as a trigger for flowering in herbaceous plants and for increased fruit and seed production \[ [112](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B112-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Resprouting is one of the main strategies employed by most species to recover after a fire \[ [113](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B113-remotesensing-18-01870), [114](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B114-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. However, there is still limited research focused on understanding wildfires and vegetation recovery, especially regarding the complex relationship between species composition and fire regime \[ [113](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B113-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. The ability of vegetation to regenerate after a wildfire depends on several factors, including fire severity, the resistance and adaptation of the plant species present, and both previous and current land use.

Regarding forests, it was observed that a higher proportion of burned areas showed significant decreasing trends in NDVI values in different types of formations (evergreen coniferous forests, evergreen broadleaf forests, deciduous broadleaf forests, and mixed forests) compared to areas with increasing trends. These findings demonstrate that several forests do not recover after fires. There is a knowledge gap regarding the timing of vegetation recovery after fires and the key factors that influence the process. Globally, estimates of post-fire vegetation productivity recovery (2004–2021) have been made, identifying that grasslands and shrublands had shorter recovery periods compared to the longer recovery time of forest and savanna formations, with differences between hemispheres \[ [30](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B30-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. In North America, mixed patterns of post-fire recovery have been recorded in forests in the western United States (1986–2017), and it was found that, in some areas, the leaf area index had recovered to 60–70% of pre-fire levels 25 years later \[ [115](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B115-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. In addition, the rate of post-fire vegetation regeneration is conditioned by various factors. These factors are related to the characteristics of the fire, environmental conditions, natural history of plant species \[ [2](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B2-remotesensing-18-01870), [113](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B113-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. In North America, in the mountain forests of Canada, post-fire regeneration has been found to be slow, and landscape heterogeneity complicates recovery \[ [116](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B116-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Meanwhile, in the Rocky Mountains of the United States, negative effects of drought on post-fire forest recovery have been observed \[ [117](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B117-remotesensing-18-01870)\].

The Americas are home to 1624 Mha of forest area, representing 39% of the world’s forests \[ [118](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B118-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. However, deforestation rates in the Americas are alarming. In the last decade (2015–2025), South America has had the highest annual rate of net forest area loss in the world, at −4.10 Mha/year \[ [118](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B118-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. A major and complex challenge in the Americas is maintaining large areas of forest intact while reversing current trends in land-use change due to deforestation and restoring degraded forests. Fires offer the scientific community, key stakeholders, and policymakers an opportunity to reassess actions to restore the landscape. Efforts should focus on restoring vegetation affected by fires, but mainly in forested areas \[ [119](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B119-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. In this regard, it is important to consider the level of degradation of an ecosystem, as well as the time, effort, and financial investment required depending on the scale. Various restoration management approaches have been suggested, including recovery, rehabilitation, commercial reforestation/agroforestry, reforestation with native species, assisted natural regeneration, and natural regeneration \[ [102](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B102-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. In Latin America and the Caribbean (2001–2014), reforestation reversals were 10 times more common than sustained increases in forest cover \[ [120](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B120-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. In terms of reforestation actions, it is estimated that by 2025, 49.8 Mha of forests were planted in North and Central America and another 17.3 Mha in South America \[ [118](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B118-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. The experiences of restoration projects in Latin America have been based mainly on active tree planting, but it has become clear that lower-cost actions are needed, with accessible practices and monitoring strategies, and better alignment with financing \[ [121](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B121-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Nevertheless, natural regeneration, the spontaneous recovery of species after a disturbance, is increasingly being promoted as a low-cost strategy for large-scale forest landscape restoration projects.

### 4.7. Limitations

A few limitations and uncertainties of our study need to be discussed. First, an important aspect to consider in satellite monitoring is spatial resolution. Our work is entirely based on 500 m MODIS products. It has been identified that the MCD64A1 product may have negative biases due to the systematic underestimation of smaller burned areas \[ [122](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B122-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. It is suggested that in other studies, to identify both burned areas and trends in NDVI values and vegetation cover time classification, higher resolution datasets, such as Landsat or Sentinel-2, with similar or longer time series be used. Second, since the NDVI data from the MOD13A2 product are based on measurements of reflected light, caution is needed when interpreting them in terms of ecological processes. Moreover, NDVI values are used to identify the greenness of vegetation, but these values are not useful for detecting forest regeneration and carbon sinks. NDVI may provide less accurate information about the increase in forest biomass due to natural regeneration or plantations. Revegetation generally refers to an increase in the overall greenness of vegetation, often herbaceous or shrubby, in dry lands or degraded areas, while forest regrowth focuses on the structural accumulation of tree biomass, woody canopy cover, and succession. The results of post-fire vegetation trend data should be combined with LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) data, which can also enable better structural characterization of the vegetation \[ [123](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B123-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Third, due to the intercontinental extent of the study area, it was not possible to provide validation for the MCD12Q1 product. Our analysis could be improved with phenological data from field plots in different types of land cover, particularly to better understand the spectral differentiation between woody canopies and herbaceous cover. Fourth, our study does not identify human restoration actions such as agroforestry, silvopasture, or forest plantations \[ [124](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/18/12/1870#B124-remotesensing-18-01870)\]. Future studies should utilize this type of field information at the continental level, which will allow for the differentiation of ecological processes of natural regeneration under uncontrolled conditions from other types of assisted restoration interventions or productive practices.

## 5\. Conclusions

This study has evaluated spatiotemporal vegetation trends in burned areas across the Americas through a temporal analysis from 2001 to 2024, with emphasis on country or territory, ecoregion, and land cover type. The major findings of this study can be summarized as follows:

i.

Fires have significantly impacted 44 countries or territories across the Americas, with a notable concentration in the central-eastern region of South America, demonstrating not only an extensive area affected but also a high recurrence of fires, underscoring the urgent need to strengthen regional fire management and prevention strategies.

ii.

Fires have affected 269 ecoregions in the Americas, with the greatest extent and highest recurrence observed in regions such as the Cerrado, Llanos, and Pantanal, highlighting the vulnerability of these ecosystems and the critical need to strengthen targeted fire management and prevention efforts at the ecoregional level.

iii.

Most burned areas in the Americas have not undergone changes in land cover, but there have been significant conversions, mainly between non-forest and forest to non-forest types, while fire recurrence has been particularly high in the savannas and grasslands of the Cerrado, underscoring the importance of fire management strategies tailored to these ecosystems.

iv.

Increasing and decreasing NDVI trends were observed in 34.8% and 21.4% of burned areas, respectively, with the largest increases concentrated in Brazil, Argentina, and United States, while the most notable decreases were found in Brazil, Canada, and United States, underscoring the spatial variability of post-fire vegetation dynamics and the importance of strengthening country-level monitoring and restoration initiatives.

v.

Increasing NDVI trends were predominant in ecoregions such as Cerrado, Llanos, and Humid Chaco, while notable decreases were found in Dry Chaco, Cerrado, and Boreal Shield. These patterns underscore the marked spatial heterogeneity of post-fire vegetation dynamics across ecoregions and highlight the need for regionally tailored fire management and ecosystem monitoring strategies.

vi.

Increasing NDVI trends after fire were mainly observed in savannas, grasslands, and wooded savannas—vegetation types naturally adapted to fire—while notable areas of both increasing and decreasing trends also occurred in broadleaf evergreen forests, which are not fire-adapted, underscoring the contrasting post-fire responses among land cover types and the urgent need to focus fire management and restoration in sensitive forest ecosystems.

The results described in this study contribute to a better understanding of the dynamics of the ecoregions of the Americas, enabling the development of appropriate and effective restoration strategies and the urgent need to establish appropriate policies and measures for comprehensive fire management tailored to different types of ecosystems. Furthermore, it is established that burned areas in different ecoregions show trends of a continuous regeneration process. Spatial identification of natural vegetation change trends post-fires is a fundamental step to implement strategies and public policies to ensure ecological restoration. Moreover, given that the costs of restoration actions are high, governments must work together to prevent these ecosystems from burning again.
