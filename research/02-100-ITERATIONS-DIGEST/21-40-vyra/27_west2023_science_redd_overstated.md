## Editor’s summary

Reducing emissions from deforestation and forest degradation (REDD) projects are intended to decrease carbon emissions from forests to offset other carbon emissions and are often claimed as credits to be used in calculating carbon emission budgets. West _et al_. compared the actual effects of these projects with measurable baseline values and found that most of them have not reduced deforestation significantly, and those that did had benefits substantially lower than claimed (see the Perspective by Jones and Lewis). Thus, most REDD projects are less beneficial than is often claimed. —H. Jesse Smith

## Abstract

Carbon offsets from voluntary avoided-deforestation projects are generated on the basis of performance in relation to ex ante deforestation baselines. We examined the effects of 26 such project sites in six countries on three continents using synthetic control methods for causal inference. We found that most projects have not significantly reduced deforestation. For projects that did, reductions were substantially lower than claimed. This reflects differences between the project ex ante baselines and ex post counterfactuals according to observed deforestation in control areas. Methodologies used to construct deforestation baselines for carbon offset interventions need urgent revisions to correctly attribute reduced deforestation to the projects, thus maintaining both incentives for forest conservation and the integrity of global carbon accounting.

Avoided deforestation conservation projects necessitate comparisons between observations in protected forests and what-would-have-happened if the project did not exist (often referred to as the baseline, or counterfactual scenario) \[1\]. Nevertheless, it is imperative that forest carbon projects deliver the carbon emission reductions they report, and there are various methods that can be used to estimate these counterfactual scenarios. West et al. \[2\] used the synthetic control method to choose pools of controls with similar covariate structure to _avoided unplanned deforestation conservation project_ areas; they do not consider other methodologies such as _planned avoided deforestation_, or _improved forest management_. Synthetic controls were calculated from the mean deforestation rate of pools, weighted by covariate similarity with

the project. We agree that the synthetic control method could potentially improve the evaluation of forest carbon projects. However, synthetic controls are also vulnerable to “gaming” or bias through inappropriate donor pool design/sampling and such econometric statistical methods require further context-specific refinement \[1\] before they can improve upon existing approaches.

West et al. \[2\] claim that most forest carbon projects overestimate avoided deforestation or do not significantly reduce deforestation. These conclusions are poorly supported by their analysis because:

- The implemented synthetic control approach can suffer from bias \[3\] due to substantial and incompatible differences between deforestation drivers, forest type and (bio)geography that are not adequately captured by the structure of covariates that are used to form the synthetic control. Important covariates are ignored, such as distance to roads and rivers \[4, 5\], fire risk \[6\], or indicators of forest structure \[7\] that predict biomass, timber value and logging effort. Using the authors’ code, we extracted the locations of controls used to create the synthetic control for project-856 (Figure 1), located in Colombia’s Pacific-montane-forest. No weighted donors were in the same political jurisdiction or ecoregion as the project and the highest weighted donor (0.46) was in the Amazon, 900 km away. We have also attempted to reproduce the synthetic controls for all considered project areas; interactive maps of these locations are presented at https://permian-global-research.github.io/science-letter-west-et-al/.
- Calculations derived from Global Forest Change (GFC) \[8\] inherit known sensitivity and accuracy issues \[9, 10\]. It is inappropriate to compare GFC-based calculations with those reported by projects, which are better calibrated for local contexts, because meaningful comparisons require deforestation rates to be derived using identical methods. Further, it is accepted that GFC is inappropriate for site-level deforestation assessment, in isolation \[10, 11\].
- West et al. \[2\] do not demonstrate that synthetic control counterfactuals are more accurate than project methods, undermining their ability to interpret differences between the synthetic control and project-reported deforestation rates.

The adoption of econometric methods to evaluate the eﬀicacy of voluntary carbon projects is an important development in the field \[1\], yet these methods need improvement. Critically, the drivers of deforestation are complex and highly variable across space and time. We believe that there is a need to more carefully consider the connections between spatial and econometric statistical techniques, in the context of remote sensing, to maximise the accuracy and utility of these methods \[13\].

No method of reconstructing a counterfactual can claim to represent absolute truth. In order to improve the evaluation of avoided deforestation conservation projects, one potential way forward could be to adopt a simulation framework to benchmark the eﬀicacy of synthetic controls alongside alternative methods. Simulations to reconstruct counterfactuals on non-project areas will provide quantitative metrics for counterfactual reconstruction accuracy. We hope this letter will stimulate further discussion on this topic and encourage the development and adoption of improved inference methods in voluntary carbon projects.

###### A critical review of West et al. (2023)

Maren Pauly

- Director of Evaluation & Research
- Everland

William Crosse

- VP Impacts
- Everland

Joshua Tosteson

- CEO
- Everland

The authors applied a synthetic control method to select a series of control areas based on a set of matching covariates prior to REDD+ project start. We find the application of this methodology and assembly of underlying data was flawed for a few key reasons - challenging the robustness of the authors’ conclusions. Key issues include:

- The systematic overstatement of both the baselines under which REDD+ projects operate, and the emissions reductions that are credited to projects
- The selection of control areas that do not match the baseline scenarios of the projects resulting in the perception of low project area risk
-  Low quality geospatial data used to estimate deforestation without presenting uncertainty
- Location of control areas were not reported, making validation and replication of findings impossible

Overall, we found at least one significant methodological failing by the authors for each project analysis presented, which materially impacted the result. We therefore question the reliability of the individual project results as well as the broader conclusions drawn therefrom.

**1\. The authors systematically overstate both the baselines under which REDD+ projects operate, and the emissions reductions that are credited to projects**

_**1.1 Inaccurate reporting of validated project baseline data resulting in systematic overstatement of actual project baseline deforestation rates**_

An important foundation of this study’s methodology is comparing the actual deforestation in the selected controls with the predicted deforestation in the project’s baseline scenario. The authors listed the cumulative baseline deforestation in hectares for each analyzed project within Table S10 and visualized the data in Figure 3 (up to 2020 or during the last year of the project’s baseline scenario). We cross-checked this data presented with the values reported in the Project Description Document (PDD) of each project to collect the total annual and cumulative deforestation area data predicted for each PA during the baseline period (up to 2020 or the final baseline year if early following West et al. 2023).

For every project analyzed by the authors, the projected cumulative baseline deforestation established in validated Project Design Documents (PDDs) was lower than the values reported by West et al.. Cumulative baselines were reported by the authors to be on average 46% higher than the values that are officially reported within PDDs and used as the basis for evaluating emissions reduction performance.  In most cases, this stems from the author’s choice, made without justification, to add the pre-project PA deforestation to the PDD cumulative baseline deforestation (Table S6).  This curious unexplained addition of pre-project deforestation to the projects’ actual baseline has the effect of increasing the apparent baseline used by projects for crediting.

In other cases, inaccurate data collection amplified this issue. For example, for the Madre de Dios REDD+ Project (ID 844), the actual cumulative baseline deforestation reported in the PDD is 21,982.8ha (2009-2018; PDD Table 29) but West et al. recorded a value of 125,501ha (equal to the sum of the cumulative column in PDD Chart 25).

Inaccurate reporting of project baselines by the authors results in their use of significantly higher baseline deforestation rates for analysis than the projects actually operate under and generate avoided emissions in relation to. As discussed below, this effect is compounded by analytic choices made by the authors in applying the SCM, for example, the authors’ frequent omission of covariates known to be the most important indicators of deforestation risk regionally.

_**1.2. Overestimation of actual emissions reductions that are credited to projects in relation to their baselines**_

When investigating whether the emissions reductions for which projects are credited are credible, the authors calculate the total emissions reductions from the baseline deforestation rate up to the last available baseline year: “a simplified approach to estimate such volumes by assuming a linear, per-hectare relationship between the baseline deforestation adopted by projects and their reported ex-ante volume of credits to be generated through 2020”. This simplified approach fails to factor in deductions from gross ex-ante credit volumes that reduce the net volume of verified and issuable credits by projects.

Verified emissions reductions reported by projects for a given monitoring period are first  deducted based on, based on emissions from monitored deforestation within the project’s defined leakage area. After leakage deduction, a percentage of the remaining net emissions reductions is withheld and deposited into a system-wide buffer pool based on a non-permanence risk assessment. Based on our analysis of \[53\] AUD REDD+ projects, over 11% of the credits generated have been deposited into the buffer pool and are unavailable for issuance.  By not considering these mandatory discounts the authors substantially overestimate the actual emissions reductions that can be credited to the projects.  This was easily avoidable as the authors could have amalgamated the actual verified credits publicly available in project monitoring and verification reports.

Of the 17 projects with emissions reductions estimates in the publication (Table S10), West et al. overestimated the actual emissions reductions in 12 cases (below Tables 1,2). On average, cumulative emissions reductions were overestimated by 978,491tCO2e per project, with a total overestimation of >17.6 million tCO2e. As such, the conclusion that projects are credited for more emissions than they achieve is based on a systematic overestimate of the actual emissions reductions for which they can be credited.

**2\. The authors selected control areas that did not match the baseline scenarios of the projects, resulting in the perception of low project risk**

We find a series of faults with the methodology underlying the selection of synthetic control areas. The authors utilized a series of covariates to statistically match potential control areas with the project area: historic deforestation (area of interest and buffer), tree cover, travel time to urban centers, elevation, slope, protected area cover, indigenous area cover, concession cover (mining, logging and palm oil), precipitation and soil fertility. Notably missing from this list of covariates are four variables known to be important predictors of deforestation risk: road network (distance to roads), rivers (distance to navigable rivers), population density and distance to previous deforestation (Aguilar-Amuchastegui et al. 2014; Barber et al. 2014). Furthermore, the authors did not require the control areas to be located within the same region as the projects, and thus they could conceivably be located at the other end of the country. Notwithstanding the fact that the study misses these important covariates, the authors weighed each covariate within their model to find the highest statistical match without: (1) applying a threshold on important covariates or (2) prioritizing weighting based on covariates that are most strongly associated with deforestation within a given project context.

_**2.1. Land tenure statuses ignored**_

Certain covariates - including economic concession cover and protected area cover - should be set to a specific threshold to ensure control areas are limited to those that strongly match with the project baseline scenario in terms of land tenure. For example, if a project would be an extractive concession in the baseline scenario, all of the control areas should also be extractive  concessions of the same variety (e.g. logging, palm oil, agriculture) for an equitable match.  Two of the avoided planned deforestation (APD) projects analyzed by the authors (IDs 934, 1359) were logging concessions in their baseline (avoided planned deforestation) - taken over by conservation concessions for the establishment of REDD+ projects. Despite the projects being 100% logging concessions in the baseline, the authors selected synthetic controls that had 0% logging concession coverage. Naturally, the deforestation rates of non-concession forest lands selected as controls are likely to be substantially lower than actively managed extractive concessions, where deforestation is a result of the implementation of a concession management plan.

On the other hand, protected status was not required to match between the control and project area in all cases - for example, for projects 844 and 1392, the project areas were under 0% protected area coverage, but were matched with control areas that had 53% and 69% legal protection, respectively. Whilst illegal deforestation still occurs in protected areas, it is typically to a much lesser degree than adjacent unprotected forests (Graham et al. 2021).

In the above mentioned cases, the authors selected a very low weighting for the relevant land tenure covariate, meaning that land tenure had minimal impact on the statistical matching quality as represented by the authors. It is clear from these examples that the authors did not investigate the exact baseline scenarios on a project-by-project basis to determine optimal control areas. If the baseline scenario is unknown, a reasonable control area to match that scenario cannot be established. All together, we found >50% land tenure mismatch in 11 of 19 projects (Table 1 below).

_**2.3. Control areas at lower background deforestation risk than matched project areas**_

Another important covariate that was not adequately matched was deforestation risk. Ideally, distance to previous deforestation would be the variable of choice (Aguilar-Amuchastegui et al. 2014; Barber et al. 2014) for avoided unplanned deforestation (AUD) REDD+ projects. In many cases, AUD REDD+ projects are at imminent risk of forest loss via a nearby deforestation frontier. For example, the Keo Seima REDD+ project in Cambodia is at imminent risk of forest loss from an incoming wave of illegal deforestation from the southwest which has already caused significant deforestation in the adjacent Snuol Wildlife Sanctuary (Graham et al. 2021; Riggs et al. 2020) resulting in the dissolution of protected status in 2018 (Ford et al. 2020). Instead, the authors selected average and cumulative deforestation _in the project area_ and a buffer zone.

In any case, realistic controls should have rates of buffer deforestation that are similar to those in project area buffers. However, in 12 of the 17 AUD projects analyzed by the authors, the project area buffer deforestation rate was higher than the control area buffer deforestation (Table 1 below) - demonstrating that the controls are at far less deforestation risk than the projects. For example, in the Jaguar Amazon REDD+ Project in Peru (ID 2278), the deforestation within the buffer around the project was 749.9ha/year, which is 3-fold higher than the buffer deforestation around the three selected control sites (248.5ha/year). Within the study period, this resulted in a total PA buffer deforestation of 5171ha, which is nearly 4-fold higher than the total control buffer deforestation (1376ha). We found substantially higher (>40%) annual project buffer deforestation rates compared to control deforestation in four project cases.

The chosen method to validate the control areas introduced further bias into the analysis. Once the controls were selected, they were validated by comparing the historic rate of deforestation within the controls to the rate of deforestation within the project area. While project areas are typically selected within high risk areas, their exact geographic area must by definition be 100% forest during the decade before project implementation (VCS, 2020). Thus, one would expect very low forest loss rates leading up to the project start within the Project Area otherwise they would not be eligible as a REDD+ intervention area. Given this, using historic project area deforestation rates as a validation will ensure all controls have not been under recent deforestation threat. In itself this would not pose a challenge to the analysis, but when coupled with the discrepancy in the buffer deforestation risk, it results in matching of areas that tend to be at systematically lower risk of deforestation than the project area. Instead, validating the model with distance to previous deforestation would have been a more robust approach.

**3.Low quality geospatial data used to estimate deforestation without presenting uncertainty**

The authors utilized an open source geospatial dataset (Hansen et al. 2013) for estimating project and control area deforestation used in the selection of control sites (Tables A1-A26), validation of the model (Table S3) and the estimation of carbon offsets based on the synthetic controls (Table S5). However, this dataset is known to have low certainty in some landscapes - particularly Sub-Saharan Africa (error: ±79%) and the Democratic Republic of the Congo (error: ±65%)(Tyukavina et al. 2015); whereas projects are required to utilize far superior geospatial imagery in addition ground-truthing for baseline setting and monitoring. Furthermore, project developers are required to discount the net emissions reductions by the error of geospatial data if it exceeds 15% as a conservative measure (VCS, 2020).

Thus, we believe the use of Hansen et al. (2013) data - without considering and adequately communicating the potential uncertainties in modeling and monitoring deforestation - results in compounding error when comparing observed deforestation in synthetic control areas using Hansen et al (2013) to cumulative baseline deforestation that are established using imagery and methods much more fit for purpose. Given the high error rates in Sub-Saharan Africa and the Democratic Republic of the Congo, the 5 projects analyzed from these regions (Project IDs: 1325, 1897, 1900, 934 and 1395) do not have reliable results.

**4\. Location of control areas were not reported, making validation and replication of findings impossible**

In an ideal scenario, the authors would have mapped the selected control areas alongside the project areas to demonstrate that they are located within socio-economic and biophysical regions that are similar to the project area in question; as required by the VCS standard when project developers when they select a Reference Area as a control. Without this transparency, it is impossible to verify the reasonableness of the control sites, which is the underpinning of the study’s methodology and findings.

**Conclusions**

In conclusion, we find a series of methodological flaws in the West et al. (2023) article that have the potential to significantly impact the findings:

- Inaccurate reporting of validated project baseline data resulting in a systematic overstatement of project baseline deforestation rates
- Emissions reductions credited to the REDD+ projects were overstated for 71% of projects when compared to actual verified emissions reductions that can be used by projects for credit issuance, with a net overestimation of >17.6 million tCO2e
- A series of covariates known to strongly influence deforestation risk were not included in the analysis, whilst many covariates used by the authors were poorly matched

  - Land tenure status was not adequately matched in 58% of analyzed projects
  - Roads, navigable rivers, population density and distance to previous deforestation were not considered, resulting in selection of synthetic control areas that do not reflect realities on the ground.

- The study does not communicate the data-related uncertainties in modeling and monitoring deforestation using Hansen et al. (2013), which is widely known to perform poorly in many contexts, including for Tanzania’s dryland, mosaic forests.
- The selected control areas from 75% of the AUD projects were under less threat than the project areas according to buffer deforestation; 24% of which were under significantly less threat

We found at least one of the serious methodological problems described herein for each project analysis presented, compromising the reliability of all individual project results and the broader conclusions on which they are based. Together, West et al’s methodological choices push their analysis toward a broad conclusion, that the analyzed REDD+ projects accrue more carbon emission reduction credits than they have actually achieved, which is based on a highly questionable scientific foundation.
