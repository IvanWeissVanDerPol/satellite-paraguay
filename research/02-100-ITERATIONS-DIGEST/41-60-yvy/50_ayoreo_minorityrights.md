# Ayoreo in Paraguay

[Return to world map](https://minorityrights.org/world-map/)

## **Profile**

There are approximately 4,000 Ayoreo, who are a trans-national people living in Paraguay and Bolivia. In Paraguay they live mainly in the central Chaco region. Traditionally the Ayoreo are hunter-gatherers; today many work on large cattle ranches, often for less than the minimum wage. Ayoreo were targeted by missionaries and removed to mission stations, a practice supported by cattle ranchers that then moved in to take their lands.

## **Background**

The name Ayoreo means ‘true people’ and refers to their way of life as hunters and gatherers. Ayoreo refer to other hunter-gatherers as ‘other true men’; they call settled populations, whether indigenous or non-indigenous, _cojñone_, which translates as ‘people devoid of correct thinking’. The Ayoreo language belongs to the Zamuco linguistic family and is similar to the language spoken by the Chamacoco people. (This and the paragraphs that follow draw on the web resources of Iniciativa Amotocodie, an NGO set up to protect the rights of Ayoreo in voluntary isolation.)

The Ayoreo people move across a very large territory spanning over 30 million hectares along the northern Great Chaco Americano between the Paraguay, Pilcomayo, Parapetí and Río Grande Rivers. Ayoreo lands do not extend to riverbank areas belonging to other indigenous peoples. Thus, from north to south, their territory stretches from the Chiquitanía mountain ranges in Bolivia to the area now occupied by Mennonite colonies in the Central Chaco of Paraguay.

Paraguay and Bolivia are the only countries outside the Amazon region where small Ayoreo communities, known locally as _silvícolas_, still reside. On the Bolivian side, the Kaa Iya National Park was established to provide protection for isolated Ayoreo communities moving across the border.

Before contact, the Ayoreo people were organized into more than fifty local groups that roamed extensive and distinct areas. Each local group enjoyed a high degree of political autonomy with independent leadership. Depending on the needs and circumstances of nomadic life, local groups could divide into subgroups and temporarily separate, only to reunite later. The most important unit of social and economic organization is the _jogasüi_, the extended family, which also included other friendly families. Relationships among many of these local groups, as well as with other indigenous peoples and settler communities, often involved conflict.

Neither the local groups nor their territories should be considered permanent. According to Iniciativa Amotocodie, Ayoreo society embraces the disappearance of groups and the formation of new ones, unions between different groups and changes in their names, often resulting in subsequent changes to the demarcation of group territory. This social structure is very fluid and has typically enabled the Ayoreo people to adapt to shifting environmental conditions.

Apart from a few small communities that continue to live in isolation, the majority of Ayoreo people reside outside the forest and in permanent settlements. As of early 2023, Iniciativa Amotocodie recorded 31 recognized Ayoreo communities in Paraguay: 15 in the Alto Paraguay department and 16 in Boquerón. The total number of Ayoreo in Paraguay and Bolivia remains around 4,000 people, half of whom live in Paraguayan territory.

## **Current issues**

Sedentary Ayoreo reside in informal settlements with limited access to clean water and face extreme vulnerability. Having been deprived of their traditional territories, these communities have undergone abrupt changes in their way of life and have been thrust into poverty and marginalization. Most of the local groups from the past have disappeared. In Paraguay, only three Ayoreo groups remain today: Garaygosode, Totobiegosode and Guidaigosode. In today’s sedentary settlements, members of different Ayoreo clans coexist alongside each other.

The Paraguay government has recognized the cultural heritage of the Ayoreo indigenous people in initial contact (official status still pending). The government recognizes the existence of Ayoreo in isolation within the protection framework of the Defensores del Chaco National Park. Despite efforts to recognize isolated indigenous peoples, the Paraguayan state lacks legislation and public bodies dedicated specifically to the protection of indigenous peoples in isolation and initial contact.

Moreover, the Defensores del Chaco National Park constitutes a small fraction of the original Ayoreo territory and represents a portion of the Paraguayan Chaco Biosphere Reserve. Meanwhile, indigenous organizations within Paraguayan civil society recognize the existence of two confirmed isolated groups: Jonoineurasade and Ayoreo Totobiegosode.

According to the NGO Iniciativa Amatocodie, there are no standardized procedures for the proactive demarcation and registration of indigenous territories in Gran Chaco. At best, some rights over lands occupied by sedentary indigenous peoples are recognized in a very limited manner. The prevailing approach has been to delimit lands not occupied by other owners when indigenous communities demand it.

The state body responsible for the surveys and protection of indigenous territories is the National Indigenous Institute (INDI). In practice, INDI simply maintains a land registry in the name of indigenous peoples and those owned by INDI that should be transferred to sedentary indigenous communities. However, INDI itself has lost rights over lands allocated in its name due to political, legal and administrative inaction, leading to occupation of these lands by third parties.

In 2018, an inter-agency committee was established for the implementation of a protocol for indigenous peoples in isolation and/or initial contact within the Ayoreo Totobiegosode Natural and Cultural Heritage (PNCAT) zone in the Department of Alto Paraguay.

**_Isolated Ayoreo communities_**

The Ayoreo are the only indigenous people in the Americas outside the Amazon Basin with members living in isolation. The central part of the territory is today the last continuous area of relatively untouched and extensive native forest of the Gran Chaco Americano, with comparatively little intrusion by external economic interests. The territories of isolated Ayoreo communities coincide with this last bastion of old-growth forest, since its lower degree of penetration provides conditions of greater protection. The area includes five Public Protected Areas or National Parks in Paraguay and two in Bolivia.

Although no outsiders have direct contact with these groups, their existence has been documented through signs of their presence, such as footprints. Isolated Ayoreo groups have also been spotted at a distance.

The Ayoreo people face multiple threats to their self-determination and subsistence. The rapid disappearance of their territory due to deforestation, mining, cattle ranching, monoculture and infrastructure projects, among others, exposes them to situations of forced contact. Most of the territory currently home to uncontacted communities is formally owned by private landowners and businesses. Only a very small portion of Ayoreo territory is preserved through national parks or protected areas.

For Ayoreo communities in isolation, it is increasingly challenging to traverse their traditional annual migration routes to access habitats that are essential for their existence and to maintain contacts between groups. In recent years, due to the voracious advance of extractivism and major infrastructure projects, there has been an increase in forced displacement for these communities. Driven by national policies promoting activities which exacerbate deforestation, Paraguayan landowners are increasingly encroaching upon the territories of uncontacted indigenous communities to establish cattle ranches. Land use proposals by private landowners, including Environmental Impact Assessments (EIA), are often approved by the authorities without adequately considering the presence and survival of silvícolas in this region.

Missionary groups continue to operate in the area, attempting to force contact with these communities to integrate them into mainstream society. In Paraguay, missionaries are encouraged by landowners and businesses aiming to remove isolated Ayoreo communities from their territories. Contact with outsiders, including missionaries, can lead to deaths following outbreaks of serious illnesses. Moreover, the separation of Ayoreo from their lands and traditional way of life is usually followed by impoverishment, vulnerability and marginalization. Iniciativa Amotocodie and other NGOs denounce this denial of Ayoreo culture as ethnocide.
